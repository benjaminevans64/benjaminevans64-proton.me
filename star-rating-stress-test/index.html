Rating Stress Test: how much can a star rating really bear?

Type in the star counts behind any rating. See the headline average next to a sample-size-adjusted score, push the rating with extra one-star reviews, then ask one of three AI analysts to explain what the numbers do and do not tell you.

How the Rating Stress Test works

  1. Enter the star counts

    Type how many reviews sit at each star level for the rating you are weighing up, or load one of the examples.

  2. Stress the rating

    Add imaginary one-star reviews with the slider and watch how far the headline average moves. Fragile ratings drop quickly.

  3. Read the markers

    The gold dot is the headline average. The white diamond is the same rating adjusted for sample size. A wide gap means the sample is small.

  4. Ask an AI analyst

    Choose Margo, Ines or Theo for a short plain-English reading of this exact distribution and three things to check before you book.

Four rules for reading any star rating

  • Sample size comes first

    Four and a half stars from 15 reviews and four and a half stars from 1,500 reviews are not the same claim. The larger sample is far harder to move and far more likely to reflect typical experience.

  • Look at the shape, not just the average

    Two ratings can share the same average. One is a steady lean toward 4 and 5 stars; the other is a split between devoted fans and unhappy customers. The bars show which one you are looking at.

  • Perfect can be thin

    A clean sheet of 5-star reviews is easy to get with a small sample. Real services with many customers almost always collect some 3-star and 1-star comments.

  • A rating cannot show who or when

    Star counts do not say who reviewed, when, whether the reviewer was invited, or whether low scores were filtered. Those are the questions to ask before trusting any number.

Three AI analysts, one rating

Margo is careful with numbers and says how much weight a sample can carry. Ines looks at what a rating hides and which questions to ask. Theo uses everyday comparisons and no statistics vocabulary. All three are AI writing styles, not people, and none of them can verify that a set of reviews is genuine.

Rating Stress Test questions

Is this an official rating of a platform?

No. Rating Stress Test is a calculator with an AI explainer. It only works with the star counts you type in. It does not look anything up, rank any service or say whether a company is good.

What is the adjusted score?

It is the headline average pulled toward a neutral 3.5 as if 20 neutral reviews had been added. A rating built on 12 reviews moves a lot; one built on 2,000 barely moves. The gap between the two markers on the ruler shows how much the sample size matters.

What does the cautious positive share mean?

It is the low end of a 95 percent confidence range for the share of reviews that give 4 or 5 stars (a Wilson interval). With few reviews the range is wide, so the cautious figure sits well below the raw percentage.

Does a spotless 5.0 mean a service is great?

Not by itself. A perfect score on a dozen reviews says very little, and a near-perfect score on thousands of reviews is unusual enough to be worth asking how reviews are collected and whether low scores are shown. It is a prompt to look closer, not proof of anything.

Can the AI tell me if reviews are fake?

No. A set of star counts cannot show who wrote the reviews or when. The AI points to patterns worth a second look and suggests what to check, but it never labels reviews as fake.

Are the three AI voices real people?

No. Margo, Ines and Theo are three AI writing styles: numerate, sceptical and plain-spoken. They explain the same numbers in different ways, so it helps to try more than one.

Is the information I type stored?

The star counts and the optional note are sent only to write the explanation. Please leave out names and private details.
